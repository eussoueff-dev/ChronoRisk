Sources

  • Spiegelhalter D. & Blastland M. The Norm Chronicles: Stories and Numbers About Danger (2013). Micromort values for travel and sports.
  • Spiegelhalter D. "Using speed of ageing and 'microlives' to communicate the effects of lifetime habits and environment." BMJ 2012;345:e8223. Microlife values for exercise, diet, smoking, alcohol, sedentary time.
  • Howard R.A. (1980s), decision analysis work that introduced the micromort.
  • U.S. Social Security Administration, Office of the Chief Actuary. Actuarial Life Table, 2021 period life table used in the 2024 Trustees Report. Annual death probability q(x) by sex.
  • Divers Alert Network and US Parachute Association fatality statistics, as summarised in the sources above.

Assumptions

  • Daily baseline = q(x) ÷ 365.25 × 1,000,000 micromorts, with q(x) linearly interpolated between whole ages. "Average" uses the mean of the male and female q(x).
  • If the embedded table is unavailable, q(x) falls back to a Gompertz–Makeham curve, q(x) = A + B·ec·x, fitted by log least squares to the same table for ages 18–90 (RMS log error ≈ 10%).
  • Risks are combined as 1 − ∏(1 − pi) rather than added, with each unit treated as an independent event.
  • The baseline already contains average everyday exposure, so entering your normal commute would double-count it.
  • Microlives are summed, then a soft cap is applied: the effect is linear up to +6 / −10 per day, then shrinks smoothly toward a ceiling of +10 / −25. Real dose–response curves flatten, and averages for extreme combinations are not reliable.
  • Microlife values describe adults keeping a habit for many years. They are associations from population studies, not guaranteed causal effects.
  • Confidence: High = large, consistent data; Medium = reasonable data with notable uncertainty; Low = rough or contested estimate.
ChronoRisk is for education only. Values are population averages, not medical advice; individual risk differs. Your inputs are stored only in this browser (localStorage).
